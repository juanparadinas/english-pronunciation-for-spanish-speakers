# What is "accent"?

> Accent is not just "how you pronounce the sounds" nor just "the melody". It
> is the stable combination of segmental and prosodic features that makes your
> way of speaking recognisable. Prosody is part of pronunciation, not a
> separate thing.

## 1. The three layers

The common claim "pronunciation matters, accent doesn't" is confused, because
it treats accent as pure melody. Separate three layers instead:

| Layer | What it includes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Segmental | Phonemes: vowels, consonants | *ship* vs *sheep* |
| Suprasegmental (prosody) | Stress, rhythm, intonation, pauses, pitch range, vowel reduction | *I didn't say he STOLE it* shifts meaning with the stressed word |
| Accent | The socially recognisable combination of both | SSBE, General American, Andalusian, Galician |

Prosody is not a phoneme, but it is part of pronunciation. L2 research groups
stress, rhythm and intonation inside pronunciation and treats them as central
to intelligibility. So "accent" is a package, not melody alone.

## 2. Pitch range is not the whole of musicality

Pitch range is the distance between the high and low tonal levels a speaker
uses. It is very perceptible, but it is only one component. Musicality also
comes from:

- Rhythm: how unstressed syllables are compressed.
- Stress placement: which syllable and which word gets the tonal peak.
- Intonation contours: rises, falls, fall-rises, and their pragmatic meaning.
- Tempo and articulation: speed, pauses, linking, reduction.
- Voice quality: laryngeal tension, resonance, nasality.

Two speakers with a similar pitch range can still sound very different.

## 3. Why the "fingerprint" hits first

We often recognise an accent before analysing a single sound. The brain
processes global patterns of rhythm, intonation, tempo and stress distribution
very fast. A non-native speaker with good segmental pronunciation is still
identifiable immediately, because the rhythmic and intonational fingerprint of
their L1 crosses the whole utterance.

## 4. British sounds "more melodic": the real cause

RP/SSBE is often perceived as more melodic than General American. The shallow
explanation is "wider pitch range". The deeper cause is rhythm: English is
stress-timed and Spanish syllable-timed. Compressing unstressed syllables makes
the tonal peaks on stressed syllables more salient, which a Spanish ear hears
as extra melody. The musicality is largely a consequence of the rhythm, not an
independent property. See [stress and rhythm](stress-and-rhythm.md).

Caveat: it is not a law. An expressive American can have more tonal range than
a monotone Briton. The British/American difference is also segmental (rhotic vs
non-rhotic /r/, flap /t/, BATH vowel, GOAT diphthong). See
[British vs American differences](uk-vs-us-differences.md).

## 5. The Spanish parallel

An accent is neither melody nor phonemes alone; it is a perceptual package.

- Aragon: the Castilian accent of Aragon is prosodically very marked (rising
  intonation, lengthened stressed syllable) with few segmental changes. (Do not
  confuse this with the Aragonese language, which has its own
  segmental/lexical traits.)
- Andalusia / Extremadura: strong segmental changes (aspiration or loss of
  final and intervocalic /s/, weakening of final /r/, ceceo/seseo) on top of a
  recognisable rhythm and intonation.

In both, the immediate impression comes from rhythm, tempo and melodic contour;
the segmental changes confirm it.

## 6. Practical priority order

To sound natural and be understood, prosody has a disproportionate effect on
comprehensibility. Prioritise in this order:

1. Stress and rhythm: stress the right syllable, reduce the unstressed ones.
2. Intonation and focus: use pitch to mark new information, questions, contrast
   and politeness.
3. High-functional-load phonemes: English vowels, /r/, /l/, /θ/, /ð/, /ʃ/,
   /tʃ/, /dʒ/.
4. Final consonants and clusters: *asks*, *texts*, *months*, *world*.
5. Pitch range and musicality: a refinement, not the base.
