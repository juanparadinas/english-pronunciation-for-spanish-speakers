# Double consonants

> A doubled consonant is NOT pronounced twice. It is a spelling signal: the
> vowel before it is short (the syllable is closed). This one insight explains
> a huge amount of English spelling.

## The core rule

A double consonant closes the preceding syllable, so the vowel is short:

| Double | Short vowel | vs single (longer/named vowel) |
|---|---|---|
| dinner | /ɪ/ | diner /aɪ/ |
| hopping | /ɒ/ | hoping /əʊ/ |
| planning | /æ/ | planing /eɪ/ |
| latter | /æ/ | later /eɪ/ |
| supper | /ʌ/ | super /uː/ |
| bitter | /ɪ/ | biter /aɪ/ |
| comma | /ɒ/ | coma /əʊ/ |

So when you see a double consonant, pronounce the consonant once and keep the
vowel before it short. The doubling exists precisely to mark that short vowel.

Corollary: a single consonant between vowels often leaves the first vowel long
or named (open syllable): diner, hoping, later, super.

## Double SS — voiceless, not /z/

A doubled consonant is also a hint about voicing. Double ss is almost always
the voiceless /s/ (or /ʃ/ in -ssion), never the voiced /z/:

- /s/: pass, miss, boss, class, kiss, mess, dress, pressure, assume.
- /ʃ/ (in -ssion): mission, passion, discussion, expression, permission.

The handful of ss words that are /z/ are the rare exceptions: dessert,
scissors, possess, dissolve, hussar. See [/z/](cons-z.md). Compare a single
intervocalic s, which is frequently /z/ (easy, present, reason).

This is why "dessert" (/z/, double s but exception) vs "desert" the noun (/z/)
vs "desert" the verb are confusing; the default for ss is /s/.

## Double RR — closes the syllable, dialect notes

Double rr also closes the syllable (short vowel before it), and it interacts
with the vowel-before-r patterns:

- arr: UK tends to a short front /æ/ (carry, marry, Harry, narrow, arrow),
  the same /æ/ in American. Contrast a single ar + vowel, which gives the
  centring /eə/ (vary, Mary, various). So carry /ˈkæri/ vs vary /ˈveəri/.
- err: short /e/ before the doubled r (merry, berry, ferry, error, terrible).
- irr / orr / urr: short vowel kept (mirror, sorry, horror, hurry, current).

### urr: UK /ʌr/ vs US /ɜːr/
In a stressed closed syllable with urr, British keeps a short /ʌ/ plus r
(hurry /ˈhʌri/, current, courage), while American uses the r-coloured /ɜːr/
(/ˈhɜːri/). This is the hurry-furry split.

## Why this matters

Double consonants are one of the few truly systematic parts of English
spelling. Reading them correctly:
1. Tells you the previous vowel is short (closed syllable).
2. Tells you ss is voiceless /s/ or /ʃ/, not /z/.
3. Tells you rr closes the syllable and shapes the a/u before r.

## Common Spanish error

> The trap: pronouncing the double consonant twice (Italian-style), or
> lengthening it; or voicing ss to /z/.
> The fix: say the consonant once, keep the vowel before it short, and keep ss
> voiceless.
