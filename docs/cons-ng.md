# Consonant: /ŋ/ (NG)

> The sound at the end of "sing". It exists in Spanish only before other
> consonants, not as a word-final sound, and never with a following hard g.

## 1. What it is

/ŋ/ is a voiced velar nasal, the "ng" in "sing", "long", "thing".

Spanish contrast: Spanish has /ŋ/ only as an allophone before /k/ or /g/
(blanco, tango). It never ends a word in Spanish. Spanish speakers tend to add
a hard /g/ (sing = "sing-g") or replace it with /n/.

## 2. How to produce it

Raise the back of the tongue to the soft palate (as for /k/ or /g/) but send
the air through the nose. Crucially, do NOT release a /g/ afterwards (in most
positions).

## 3. Angle 1 — when a spelling makes /ŋ/

| Spelling | Sound | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| ng (word-final) | /ŋ/ (no g) | sing, long, thing, song, ring |
| ng + suffix | /ŋ/ (no g) | singer, singing, hanger |
| ng (medial, some) | /ŋg/ (with g) | finger, longer, anger, hungry |
| n before k/g | /ŋ/ | think, bank, uncle, drink |

## 4. Angle 2 — where the /ŋ/ sound is written

ng (final, no g): sing, long, thing, song, ring, king, bring, young, among,
morning, nothing, evening.
-inger/-inging: singer, singing, bringing, hanging.
With a hard g (/ŋg/): finger, longer, anger, hungry, England, single, stronger.
n before k: think, bank, drink, uncle, monkey, thank, link.

## 5. Minimal pairs / contrasts

| Pair | Contrast |
|---|---|
| sing / sin | /ŋ/ vs /n/ |
| thing / thin | /ŋ/ vs /n/ |
| ran / rang | /n/ vs /ŋ/ |
| sinner / singer | /n/ vs /ŋ/ |
| wing / win | /ŋ/ vs /n/ |

## 6. Common Spanish error

> The trap 1: adding a hard g, so "sing" = "sing-g".
> The trap 2: replacing with /n/, so "sing" = "sin".
> The fix: back of tongue up, air through the nose, no g release at the end.
> Compare the native rule: finger has /ŋg/, but singer has only /ŋ/.
