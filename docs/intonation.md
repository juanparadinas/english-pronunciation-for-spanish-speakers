# Intonation

> Intonation, the melody of speech, carries meaning in English. The same words
> can be a statement, a question, or a signal of attitude depending on the
> pitch pattern.

## 1. Why it matters

English uses pitch to signal grammar and attitude, not just emotion. Wrong
intonation can turn a statement into a question, sound rude, or obscure the
point. Spanish uses intonation too, but the patterns differ, and English leans
on it more heavily because it is stress-timed.

## 2. Thought groups

Speech is chunked into thought groups: short meaningful phrases separated by
tiny pauses. Each group has one main stressed word (the nucleus) that carries
the pitch movement.

Chunking correctly makes long sentences intelligible: "When I got home // I
realised // I had left my keys // at the office."

## 3. The main tunes

- Falling tone: statements, commands, wh-questions, finished thoughts.
  "I live in Madrid." (pitch falls on MA-drid)
- Rising tone: yes/no questions, uncertainty, "please continue".
  "Are you coming?" (pitch rises at the end)
- Fall-rise: contrast, reservation, politeness. "I LIKE it..." (but).

## 4. Nucleus placement

The most important word in a thought group gets the pitch movement. Moving the
nucleus changes meaning:
- "I didn't say she STOLE it." (someone else did / she did something else)
- The stressed word signals the focus.

## 5. Uptalk

The rising tone on statements (high rising terminal). Common in some accents
and younger speakers. Worth recognising; use with care.

## 6. Common Spanish error

> The trap: flat or Spanish-pattern intonation, or rising where English falls,
> making statements sound like questions.
> The fix: chunk into thought groups, put the pitch movement on the key word,
> and fall at the end of statements and wh-questions.
