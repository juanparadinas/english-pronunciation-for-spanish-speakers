# Priority phonemes for Spanish speakers

Not all phonemes matter equally. These are the highest-leverage ones for a
native Spanish speaker, in order.

## Vowels (top 4)

1. **Schwa /ə/**. The most important. The biggest Spanish error is not using
   it; Spanish forces a full written vowel into every syllable. Even schwa
   deletion never happens in Spanish speech. See [schwa](vowel-schwa.md).
2. **Long schwa /ɜː/**. Very specific sound; almost no Spanish native says it
   right. Appears in many common words. Spelled via vowel + R patterns:
   I+R, U+R, E+R, W+OR (and a few others). Knowing the spelling-to-sound
   mapping both ways is key.
3. **Short /ɪ/**. Does not exist in Spanish. The issue is quality, not length
   versus /iː/. No Spanish speaker pronounces it well. Many common minimal
   pairs with /iː/ make it critical (leave/live, cheese/cheat).
4. **Long back /ɑː/**. The genuinely hard one for Spanish speakers, not the
   front /æ/. The Spanish "a" is fairly front, so /æ/ (cat) is close enough;
   the long, back /ɑː/ (car, father, past) is the foreign sound. More frequent
   in SSBE than in American because of the TRAP-BATH split (past, path, class
   take /ɑː/ in SSBE, /æ/ in American). Spanish speakers also tend to add a
   rolled R in words like "car".

Note: in SSBE the long/short vowel distinction is more elastic (more evident)
than in American. IPA often marks the two dots (ː) for British and drops them
for American.

## Consonants (most important, do not exist in Spanish)

1. **R**. Very different from the Spanish R; almost no native produces the
   English R well.
2. **Dark L**. Distorts adjacent sounds and phonemes heavily. Spanish speakers
   rarely produce it.
3. **/z/**. Does not exist in Spanish. Many S/Z minimal pairs.
4. **/ʃ/ (SH)**. Very common. We can produce it, but the problem is not
   knowing which spellings trigger it (not only written SH; many endings).
5. **/v/**. Does not exist in Spanish; "b" and "v" are the same letter-sound,
   so speakers collapse /b/ and /v/. Many minimal pairs (berry/very, ban/van,
   best/vest). A distinct labiodental, not a bilabial.
6. **H**. Spanish speakers tend to say it like a Spanish J or drop it entirely.

The rest of the non-Spanish consonants matter but are lower priority. /tʃ/
(CH) exists in some Spanish regions (Río de la Plata, parts of Spain), so it
is producible; its voiced sibling /ʒ/ is much rarer in English.
