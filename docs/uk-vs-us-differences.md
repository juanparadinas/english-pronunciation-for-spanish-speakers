# British vs American differences

Reference accent is SSBE. There are 8 main pronunciation differences to
American: 5 vowels, 3 consonants. Plus grammar and vocabulary differences.

## Vowel differences (5)

1. **Short O**. British pronounces an O; American pronounces a back /ɑ/
   (a back a, like long a but not long). Exception: when followed by R.
   Biggest vowel difference. hot, lost, dock, stock.
2. **Long /ɑː/ distribution**. Not a different sound, but British uses it in
   many more words. Beyond the +R cases (long a in both), British says
   past, path, class, last with long /ɑː/; American uses /æ/.
3. **/əʊ/ vs /oʊ/** (the "home" diphthong). American pronounces it like the
   Spanish "casa" o; British replaces the o with a schwa (/əʊ/). Very
   characteristic. home, go, so, road.
4. **Centring diphthongs from final R**. Words ending in R: British does not
   pronounce the final R and substitutes a schwa. Very noticeable.
   fair, here, tour → /eə/, /ɪə/, /ʊə/.
5. **Front /æ/**. American makes it a kind of "ea" diphthong (and nasalizes
   it more, especially in the South). cat.

## Consonant differences (3)

1. **Yod drop (/j/ drop)**. American dropped the yod in many words that keep
   it in British. during, dual, tune → British /dj-/ (→ /dʒ/ by
   assimilation), American /d-/. Some common American words still keep it.
2. **Rhoticity**. American is generally rhotic; British is non-rhotic.
   Consequence: in the long schwa /ɜː/, British holds a long sound; American
   must fit the R in, so the schwa is shorter.
3. **T**. American uses the flap t in unstressed syllables (like a Spanish R):
   better, city. British uses a normal T, though some British accents use a
   glottal t far more.

## Flap T detail

- American: flap t inside the word (better, city).
- British: flap t mainly when linking words (as in "But I"); increasingly
  common but primarily at word boundaries.

## Elasticity

British is more elastic: the long/short vowel distinction is more evident.
IPA transcriptions often show the two dots (ː) for British and drop them for
American.

## Grammar / usage notes (from the reference material)

- any question/questions
- for me / to me
- at or in the end (languagetool insights article)
- take a decision / make a decision
- it's worth + gerund (not "worth to + infinitive")
- used to / usually
- make sense (not "have sense")
- I agree (not "I am agree")
- responsible for (not "responsible of")
- people are (not "people is")
- related to (not "related with")
- I / me / myself
